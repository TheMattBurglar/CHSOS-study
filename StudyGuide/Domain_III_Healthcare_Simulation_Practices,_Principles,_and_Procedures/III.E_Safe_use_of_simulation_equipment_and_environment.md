# III.E: Safe use of simulation equipment and environment

## Domain: Healthcare Simulation Practices, Principles, and Procedures (25%)

### [Status]: 🟢 Studied

### [Technician Perspective]
**Safe Use of Equipment & Environment:** Adhere to OSHA and SDS (Safety Data Sheets) for simulated fluids. Ensure 'Sharps' safety protocols match the clinical environment. Check for latex allergies and ensure all equipment is cleaned/disinfected between sessions. Three recurring exam-relevant practices:
*   **Storage of Manikins:** Store full-body manikins supine on a padded surface or in a lockable storage shelf/cart, not folded or left in prolonged flexed positions that stress joints and silicone skin. Keep away from direct sunlight and temperature extremes, which degrade silicone and latex components, and lightly powder skin surfaces before extended storage to prevent tackiness and sticking.
*   **Clearing IV Lines:** After any scenario using simulated blood or IV fluids, flush internal tubing and reservoirs with a manufacturer-approved solution (often distilled water, sometimes with isopropyl alcohol) before storage. Residue left in lines breeds bacteria and mold, clogs pump mechanisms, and is both an infection-control and equipment-longevity issue.
*   **Disinfection Procedures:** Use manufacturer-approved wipes/solutions on manikin skin between learners (many alcohol-based products degrade silicone over time); disinfect high-touch environmental surfaces (bed rails, monitors, IV poles) between sessions the same as a real unit would.

### [Clinical Application]
Physical & Psychological Safety: A trip hazard (cable) or a needle stick injury in the lab breaks the 'Fiction Contract' and can cause real harm.

### [Key Terminology]
*   **SDS (Safety Data Sheets):** Essential documents for every chemical/fluid in the lab.
*   **Fiction Contract:** The agreement between learners and facilitators to treat the simulation as real.
*   **Psychological Fidelity:** The extent to which a simulation evokes the same cognitive and emotional responses as the real-world task; safety incidents (real needle sticks, trip hazards) break both physical safety and this fidelity.
*   **Biofilm:** A residue of microorganisms that can build up inside unflushed IV tubing and fluid reservoirs, the main reason simulated lines must be cleared, not just wiped externally.

### [Exam Scenario]
**Q:** A student is accidentally stuck by a needle during a simulation. What is the first operational priority?
**A:** Follow the center's bloodborne pathogen exposure protocol (even if the needle was 'clean') and then investigate if a safety-engineered device should have been used instead.
**Q2:** A manikin has been stored folded at the hips in a supply closet for several months between courses, and its skin has developed cracking at the hip crease along with a musty smell from the fluid reservoir. What two separate safe-use failures does this reveal, and how should the operator correct them going forward?
**A2:** This is both a storage failure (prolonged flexion stressed and cracked the silicone; the manikin should have been stored supine or per manufacturer positioning guidance) and a clearing failure (the internal fluid lines were never flushed before storage, allowing microbial growth/biofilm). Going forward, establish an end-of-course checklist requiring lines to be flushed and manikins returned to correct storage position before the lab closes.

### [Notes & Lab Application]
*   Post the SDS binder in a visible, central location.
*   Use 'Safety Needles' whenever possible to mirror current clinical practice.
*   Build an end-of-day/end-of-course closing checklist that includes flushing IV lines and returning manikins to correct storage position.

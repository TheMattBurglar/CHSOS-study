Chapter 2: Who, What, and Where?

Simulation isn't just for doctors in big hospitals. It's a diverse world that starts on the street and ends in specialized operating rooms. 

For Emergency Medical Services (EMS), training often happens "in situ"—which means right in the back of a moving ambulance or on the side of a road. They use mobile labs and realistic makeup, known as moulage, to practice life-saving skills like intubation and trauma care under pressure.

In Nursing Schools, the focus is on coordination and safety. Nurses are often the "eyes and ears" of a hospital, so their simulation drills emphasize catching medication errors and monitoring patient changes. They use everything from "task trainers" (like a standalone arm for starting I-Vs) to full-body manikins in rooms that look exactly like Intensive Care Units.

Medical Students use simulation to bridge the gap between textbooks and real people. They might start with virtual reality to learn anatomy and move toward high-stakes exams using "Standardized Patients"—actors who are trained to act out specific illnesses consistently.

We also see simulation in the Military, where "Tactical Combat Casualty Care" is practiced in immersive environments that mimic battlefields, complete with the sounds and smells of combat.

Finally, even the Lay Public benefits. When you take a CPR class or a "Stop the Bleed" workshop at a community center, you are participating in healthcare simulation. Whether it's a high-tech robot or a simple plastic torso, the goal is the same: building the muscle memory needed to save a life.
